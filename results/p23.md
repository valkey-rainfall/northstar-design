## Reading

- On this load C is ahead of SEQ at every point: 1.35x at io4, 2.56x at io8, 5.49x at io16. C scales with
  IO threads (11.3, 26.3, 55.5 M); SEQ stops at about 10.1-10.3 M from io8 up.
- SEQ's limit is the main thread from io8 up: main active 96%, while the tier answers only 69% of GETs; the
  other 31% go to main. The share is the same at every io point, so it looks structural at pipeline 64. A
  likely cause (not tested): the tier runs only on a contiguous prefix of GETs and not while the client has
  commands in flight on main (fpSpeculate, src/fastpath.c:1343), so a command split across reads sends the
  rest of that pipeline to main. At io4 SEQ is limited by its IO threads (main 77%, IO 96%).
- ORIG at io8 is 1.24x SEQ. Its tier answers about 100% of GETs, main is almost idle (0%), and its 7 IO threads
  are 96% active, so ORIG is IO-thread-bound at 12.7 M, still 0.48x C at io8.
- STOCK is main-bound at 3.8-4.0 M at every point, as in Figure 1. C/STOCK (2.85x, 6.84x, 14.4x) is close
  to Figure 1 (2.8x, 6.9x, 14.6x), and C's absolute numbers are within 0.6% of Figure 1 (11.30, 26.40,
  55.78 M), so the generator change did not move server-bound cells much. This is a cross-check only.

## Fairness limits

- Pipeline 64 is the Figure 1 load, not the load the tier was tuned on (an earlier host used pipeline 16). The 69%
  tier share suggests SEQ loses more at pipeline 64 than at 16. This A/B does not show SEQ at pipeline 16.
- Upstream bases differ: STOCK and C are on the 2026-10-19 base, SEQ on 2026-10-06, ORIG on 2026-09-22.
- ORIG needs a non-default flag to turn the tier on; SEQ, C (collector thread) and STOCK use defaults or
  the documented C setting.
- Generator: cachecannon 0.0.34 (1bea936b), not the Figure 1 generator.
  (P23b below now measures SEQ at pipeline 16.)

### Reading

- The tier share depends on the pipeline. At pipeline 16 SEQ's tier answers all GETs (1.00) and main is
  idle (0%); at pipeline 64 it answers 69% and main is the limit. So the P23 SEQ ceiling of about 10 M is a

### Part 2: why SEQ's tier answers 69% of GETs at pipeline 64 and 100% at pipeline 16

Code (59674df38):
- The tier writes each speculated GET reply into the client's own reply buffer c->buf, and only if the
  whole reply fits: dplusWriteBulkReply, src/dplus.c:383-384 (`if (total > available) return 0; /* Won't fit
  -- punt */`); the failure returns 0 from dplusValidateAndReply (src/dplus.c:590-591).
- The first failure ends speculation for the rest of the read (contiguous-prefix rule, src/dplus.c:888-901);
  every later command of that read goes to main. fpSpeculate then sends c->buf and resets bufpos
  (src/fastpath.c:1343-1349).
- The fast-path owner applies main's reply-buffer policy to its clients (fpOwnerCron, src/fastpath.c:1806-1818,
  calling clientsCronResizeOutputBuffer). That policy shrinks the buffer to max(PROTO_REPLY_MIN_BYTES = 1024,
  peak + 1) when the peak is under half the size (src/server.c:1052, src/server.h:235), and grows it only when
  the peak EQUALS the buffer size (src/server.c:1054). The buffer starts at 16 KB (src/networking.c:372).
- During the prefill the replies are small (+OK), so the buffer shrinks to 1 KB. A GET 16 B reply is 23 bytes
  ($16\r\n + 16 + \r\n). 44 replies fill 1,012 bytes; the 45th (needs 1,035) does not fit and is punted. The
  peak stays at 1,012, never equal to 1,024, so the buffer never grows back.
- Other candidates checked and excluded: there is no per-read or per-batch command cap (DPLUS_BATCH_DEPTH is
  the size of a prefetch ring that is refilled, src/dplus.c:810-838); the request-size and reply-size
  commandlog thresholds are far above 16 B / 23 B; the in-flight rule (src/fastpath.c:1343) and a trailing
  partial command would give a share that is not a fixed fraction of the pipeline depth.

Prediction: at pipeline P the share is min(P, 44) / P: 1.00 at P <= 44, 44/48 = 0.917 at 48, 44/64 = 0.6875
at 64. The build has no counter for the punt reason (the DPLUS_STAT counters are compiled only with
IO_LOOKUP_OFFLOAD_STATS), so the step was located with two short the runner probes (SEQ, io-threads 8,
1 rep each, otherwise the P23 load):

| pipeline | task | share measured (fastpath_speculated / total_commands_processed) | predicted | main active % |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 16 | P23b io8 (2 tasks) | 1.001 | 1.000 | 0 |
| 32 | 2026.11.11_05.42.59.693122 | 1.0007 | 1.000 | 0 |
| 48 | 2026.11.11_05.43.01.659346 | 0.9172 | 0.9167 | 59 |
| 64 | P23 io4/8/16 (6 tasks) | 0.688 | 0.6875 | 96 (io8, io16) |

The probes are 1 rep each and are used only for the share, not for throughput. In every SEQ window
reply_buffer_expands is 0 (reply_buffer_shrinks 1 per rep), which fits a buffer that never grows.

Reading: the 31% handed to main at pipeline 64 is not a design limit of the tier. It comes from the 1 KB
reply buffer that the stock resize policy leaves in place, plus the tier's rule to punt a reply that does
not fit rather than fill the buffer. Any reply larger than about 1 KB in total per read hits the same step.
Not changed or tested: a SEQ build that writes into a larger buffer or lets the buffer grow; per the brief
SEQ's code was not changed. What was not measured directly: the buffer size itself (CLIENT LIST rbs was not
read during the tasks, to avoid touching the measured server); the 44-reply step is inferred from the shares.

### Why SEQ's tier answers few GETs on mixed loads

The tier speculates only a contiguous prefix of GETs in each read; the first non-GET command (a SET) ends the
prefix, and it and every later command of that read go to main (src/dplus.c:848, "ineligible command ends the
global prefix"; the prefix rule is described at src/dplus.c:806-826). With uniform random SETs at fraction s
and P commands per read, the expected prefix is sum_{k=1..P} (1-s)^k GETs out of P(1-s) GETs:

| mix | pipeline | predicted share | measured share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 80/20 | 16 | 3.887 / 12.8 = 0.304 | 0.300 (io8), 0.300 (io16), 0.301 / 0.300 (fixed rates) |
| 50/50 | 16 | 1.000 / 8 = 0.125 | 0.124 (io8), 0.059 (io16) |
| 80/20 | 64 | 4.0 / 51.2 = 0.078 | 0.077 |

The model fits every cell except 50/50 io16 (0.059, half the prediction); not explained here. A likely
candidate (not tested) is the in-flight rule (src/fastpath.c:1343): no speculation while the client has
commands pending on main, which matters more when main is slower per read. With 70% or more of GETs and all
SETs on main, main is the limit (97% active) in every closed-loop SEQ cell.

### Reading

- Throughput: on mixed loads SEQ gives no gain over the phased loop (C). C is 2.3-2.7x SEQ at io8 and
  5.0-5.9x at io16 (SEQ/C 0.17-0.44). SEQ is close to STOCK: +12% at 80/20, -2% to -5% at 50/50. SEQ's main
  thread executes every SET and most GETs, so SEQ does not scale with IO threads (3.49 M at io8, 3.44 M at
  io16), while C does (8.0 -> 17.2 M at 80/20).
- Latency at a fixed rate: at 2.1 M (60% of SEQ's max) C and SEQ are the same at p99 and p999 (0.434 /
  0.434 ms GET p99), and SEQ's p50 is 15% higher (0.165 vs 0.144 ms). At 3.0 M (86% of SEQ's max, 37% of C's)
  SEQ is 28% higher at GET p99 (0.600 vs 0.470 ms), and STOCK (96% of its max) is at 2.6 ms. So no latency
  gain for SEQ either; its latency rises sooner because its main thread is nearer its limit.
- Overlapping reads with writes does not help here because the tier does not overlap them: a SET in a read
  sends the rest of that read to main, so writes cut the tier's share to 30% (80/20) or 12% (50/50).

### Caveats

- Different upstream bases: C and STOCK on 2026-10-19, SEQ on 2026-10-06.
- Uniform random keys, 3 M keys, no key churn (no new keys, no expiry, no deletes), no maxmemory or eviction.
- Generator cachecannon 0.0.34 (1bea936b); do not mix with Figure 1.
- Pipeline 16 except the two pipeline-64 cells, where SEQ also has the 1 KB reply-buffer step (P23c).
- Closed-loop latency is queueing; the fixed-rate latency is the comparable one. Rates were set from step 1
  R1 values (SEQ 3.494, STOCK 3.115 M).
- For C and STOCK the GET and SET histograms are almost identical; for SEQ they differ a little (GETs that the
  tier answers do not wait for main).
